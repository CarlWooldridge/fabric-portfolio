The Verdict Loop

Turning Carl's write-back actions into a rubric that corrects itself — and a hard look at what the evaluation run actually costs to execute.

Drafted 2026-08-26 Evidence 894 logged rows, 201 applications Source Fabric JobSearch_DB, live Status for review

Three decisions are settled and specified below. Two rubric triggers get demoted from vetoes to deductions, a fourth verdict is added for the compensable middle, and the uncapped score gets recorded so that middle can be ranked. Everything else here is the machinery to keep them honest.

Decision A

Demote two vetoes

contract and consulting-by-JD-language return to being deductions. Carl overrode them 45% and 48% of the time.

Decision B

Add Review

A fourth verdict for rows where a compensable veto fired. 53% of those turn into applications.

Decision C

Record Score_Raw

The uncapped, pre-override score, logged beside Score, so capped rows stop collapsing into an unrankable pile.

Part One

The self-learning rubric

Why a weights file, not the instructions

COWORK_INSTRUCTIONS.md is 2,374 lines and ~40,700 tokens, and it holds the safety rules — the blank-gate, the dual-write discipline, the OneDrive reconciliation contract — interleaved with the scoring rules. A process that patches that prose to adjust a weight can damage a rule that has nothing to do with scoring.

Splitting them means the worst a bad learning run can do is produce bad scores, never break the write protocol. It also makes the weights computable, which the backtest step requires.

Which rules survive as vetoes

The rule is empirical, not editorial: a veto is justified only where Carl's override rate is at or near zero. That single test explains every disagreement in the log, and it matches how he describes his own decision — consulting and a bad commute still gets an application if the stack, comp and domain are interesting enough. The decision function is compensatory. Vetoes are the exception that must earn its place.

The consulting trigger splits cleanly along its two detection methods

The named-firm list is a perfect rule — 28 fired, zero overrides. The language heuristic is a coin flip, and it misfires on precisely Carl's lane: Company-285, Company-680, Company-66, Company-500, Company-178, Company-188 — Company-423-partner and IT-services shops hiring Power BI and Fabric people. One rule is doing two jobs and only one of them well.

Refinement, found while sweeping the unacted rows (2026-08-26)

A plain demotion would release too much. The seven-firm Big-N list is too short — Company-575, Company-683, Company-04, Company-135, Company-697, Company-701, Company-178, Company-678, Company-459 and Company-653 are all genuine consultancies the language heuristic was catching correctly. Demoting it wholesale hands those back as false positives.

The cleaner signal is role shape, not employer. Compare what Carl applied to at consulting-flagged employers — Power BI Developer, BI Engineer, Data Architect, Solutions Architect, Advanced Analytics Developer — against what he left untouched: Engagement Manager, Managing Consultant, Sr Delivery Consultant, Data Program Delivery Lead, Principal Analytics. Hands-on stack roles versus client-delivery roles, and the employer barely matters. Company-178 appears on both sides: he applied to its Advanced Analytics Developer role (78) and ignored its Performance Improvement Consultant role (33).

So: expand the named-firm list, and rewrite the residual heuristic to key on delivery/engagement role shape rather than generic consulting language.

The Review verdict

The intuitive version — carve out a score band and call it Review — does not survive contact with the data. Within the current-era Skip population the apply rate is flat at 18–22% from score 10 all the way to 79. Score has almost no power to locate the applications.

no filter (base rate)20%
capped score only24%
score band 50–6926%
score band 60–6927%
compensable veto fired53%

Precision — share of surfaced rows Carl actually applied to. Current era (≥ 2026-08-10), 521 negatives, 104 applications.

Keying on the compensable vetoes surfaces 64 rows and catches 34 applications — 2.6× the base rate, and a short enough list to actually read. So:

Definition

Verdict = "Review" fires when a compensable gate fired and no veto-class gate did. The compensable set is currently contract, consulting-by-language on a non-Big-N employer, and comp in the VETO_LINE–FLOOR band. It means: the negatives fired, but this one is your call, not mine.

Precedence sits between Pass and Skip. A veto-class gate (low comp under VETO_LINE, Ladders post, named consulting firm) still produces Pass and outranks Review.

Score_Raw and the capped pile

168 current-era rows sit in the 30–39 band because the lane-fit and exclusion caps flatten everything to ~35. Inside that bucket the rubric cannot rank at all — and 30 of Carl's applications are hiding in it undifferentiated. That is why "capped score only" scores 24%, barely above the 20% base rate.

Logging Score_Raw — uncapped, pre-override — makes the capped population rankable and lets a future Review rule fire on high raw score killed by a gate, which is the real pattern.

Migrated 2026-08-26 done

Both copies moved 23 → 24 columns via scripts/migrate-add-score-raw.py, backed up first, validated independently, data byte-identical to the pre-migration state. All 894 historical rows carry blank Score_Raw deliberately: of the 236 rows scoring exactly 35, only 2 retain component math in Notes, so the uncapped score is unrecoverable for the population that needed it. Blank means "not recorded at the time" — re-deriving it from a title would be fabrication.

The chain past the OneLake shortcut is manual. JD_Transform's column mapping, the SQL table, and the semantic model do not update themselves — Score_Raw stays invisible in Power BI until the dataflow is edited to carry it. The 2-minute shortcut poll does not finish this job.

How the learner runs

It proposes; Carl disposes. Nothing in this loop writes to the rubric, the log, or the database on its own authority — which matches the blank-gate discipline the rest of the workflow already enforces.

  1. Pull the authoritative actions

    scripts/fabric-pull-actions.py reads Fact_JobPostings from the Fabric semantic model — Direct Lake over the JobSearch_DB mirror, so it reflects report write-backs within about a minute. Read-only, token minted on demand from the existing az login. Output lands in scratch/, never the OneDrive root.

  2. Build the training set

    Apply the exclusions below. Anything that fails them is counted and reported, not silently dropped.

  3. Measure, don't fit

    Recompute override rate per veto, component means for applied vs. passed, and Review precision. A rule changes bucket only when its measured rate crosses the threshold — not because a regression nudged a coefficient.

  4. Backtest before proposing

    Replay the full training window under the proposed weights. Report how many of the historical disagreements would have resolved correctly, and how many previously-correct rows would break. A proposal that improves precision while destroying recall gets rejected here, not after it ships.

  5. Write the proposal, not the change

    rubric/weights.proposed.json plus an entry in rubric/learning_log.md: what changed, from what to what, the evidence, the n, and the backtest delta. Carl reviews the diff.

  6. Promote on approval only

    On Carl's yes, weights.proposed.json becomes weights.json, the old version is retained by version for rollback, and the learning log entry is marked accepted.

What the training set must exclude

Three exclusions, each of which would otherwise poison the labels:

ExclusionRowsWhy
Pursue verdicts before 2026-08-10124 Produced under the deleted 50–69 Pursue band. A rubric that no longer exists. Including them understates current precision by 32 points.
Closed-posting Pass rows~22 Reason of "No longer accepting applications", "anonymous end client", "boilerplate rejection". A closed posting cannot be applied to, so it lands permanently in the agreed column and silently inflates whatever veto it is stapled to.
Blank Carls_Action489 "Never acted on", not "correctly skipped". A passive, unverified label. Confirmed actions carry the signal.

Guardrails

  • Minimum n before any rule moves. The post-08-10 Pursue→Pass set is 11 rows. That is too thin to tune weights on, and the learner should say so rather than fit noise.
  • Cap movement per run. No single component weight moves more than a few points in one proposal, regardless of what the fit suggests.
  • The learner never touches gate G9. A people-management gate was proposed and deliberately rejected on 2026-08-10; the identifier is left vacant on purpose. The data agrees — 47% of Carl's 201 applications carry a manager, director, head, VP or lead title. People-management is a deduction with scope = 0, and it stays one.
  • Never edit safety rules. The learner's write scope is rubric/ and nothing else. The dual-write, backup, and reconciliation rules are out of bounds by construction.
  • Outcome stays out, for now. Only 19 of 77 Pursue applications and 9 of 76 Skip applications have a known outcome — and 100% of both are "Rejected". One interview in the entire dataset. Zero variance is zero signal; feeding it in would produce confident nonsense. Revisit near n ≈ 50 with real spread.

Part Two

What the run actually costs

The evaluation run is slow and expensive for a reason that has little to do with which model is driving it: roughly half the token budget is spent on deterministic file work that a stable script should be doing, and a third of the instruction load is irrelevant to the task at hand.

The single biggest finding: dedupe is not scripted

scripts/README.md instructs the operator to "extract job IDs, dedupe against JD_Evaluation_Log.csv, then run" — but no dedupe script exists. So the model does it, against a 1.2 MB / 894-row file, on every run. This is a pure set operation on Job_ID. It should never have reached a language model.

The second: the CSV writer is re-authored from scratch each run

scratch/append_rows_20260816.py is 39,306 bytes. …20260817.py is 21,241. Each is a fresh, hand-written script with every row's data inlined as Python source — roughly 10,000 output tokens per run to regenerate boilerplate that has not meaningfully changed. Output tokens are the expensive ones.

What to script — in priority order

  1. jd-dedupe.py built 2026-08-26

    Takes extracted job IDs or full JSON records, returns new / exact-dupe / probable-repost / within-batch partitions, plus a blank-field backfill plan and flagged conflicts for each dupe hit. Repost detection is fuzzy on normalized company + title similarity, since a repost rarely reuses the title verbatim. Read-only; deterministic; near-zero tokens.

  2. jd-append.py built 2026-08-26

    A stable writer taking rows as JSON. Reconcile → one backup per run → append with csv.writer → validate → mirror to OneDrive independently → validate again. Encodes the dual-write discipline in code instead of re-deriving it from prose every run, which is where that discipline is most likely to be dropped. Halts on unknown columns, already-logged IDs, embedded newlines, and any OneDrive desync in the unexpected direction.

  3. Split the instruction file done 2026-08-26

    Done: 2,542 lines became a 106-line index plus seven parts in instructions/, split by run type. A Phase B job-alert run now loads the index + four parts and skips ~880 lines — Phase G's 559-line LinkedIn protocol, the triage and audit phases, and the legacy appendix. Verified line-for-line: zero content lines lost, 16 cross-file references rewritten to name their file.

  4. jd-backfill.py

    The blank-only merge rules are fully specified and entirely mechanical: fill blanks, never overwrite, never touch judgment fields, append a dated Notes line, flag conflicts. All of that belongs in code.

On running this on Haiku

The instinct is right but the order matters. Splitting orchestration from evaluation is sound; doing it before the mechanical work is scripted would make things worse, not cheaper.

RoleRecommendationReasoning
Orchestrator
parse alerts, run scripts, assemble the summary		 Haiku — after step 2 above Genuinely mechanical once dedupe and append are scripts. Today it is hand-writing CSV surgery under a dual-write contract with a documented desync failure mode — the last thing to hand to the cheapest model. Script first, then downgrade.

gates, scoring, verdict		 Sonnet, batched 5–8 JDs per agent Tier-1/2/3 stack matching, lane classification, domain-as-deliverable and consulting-pattern detection are the judgment core. The 48% misfire rate on the consulting heuristic shows this is already the weak link.
Fan-out shape never one agent per JD Each subagent starts cold and needs the rubric — ~8,000 tokens of gates and scoring. 25 agents means loading it 25 times, which is worse than today's single cached context. Batching 5–8 amortises it and still parallelises the wall-clock.

The quality interaction worth naming

The learner in Part One is only as good as the labels underneath it. Every evaluation is a training example, and a cheaper evaluator produces noisier Verdict, Score and component values — which the loop then learns from and encodes into the weights.

Savings on the evaluator compound into the rubric in a way savings on the orchestrator never do. That is the argument for spending the budget where the judgment is and taking it out of the file plumbing instead.

On wall-clock time

Most of the elapsed time is not token generation. fetch-jds.js retries up to 5 times per posting with jittered, attempt-multiplied backoff, capped at 25 postings per run — that pacing is deliberate anti-detection behaviour and should not be tuned for speed. The fetch is already a script and already cheap in tokens. Leave it alone; the time it takes is the price of the retrieval tier working at all.

The tractable wall-clock win is parallelism in evaluation, which the batched-subagent shape above provides, not faster fetching.

Sequencing

What to do first

The two parts are independent, but one ordering is meaningfully better: the mechanical cleanup makes every subsequent learner run cheaper to execute and easier to trust.

  1. Script dedupe and append

    Largest cost reduction, zero design risk, and it hardens the dual-write discipline that currently lives only in prose.

  2. Demote the two vetoes; add Review

    Pure instruction edits plus the initial weights.json. No schema migration required, so this can ship on its own.

  3. Migrate the log for Score_Raw

    Its own change window, both copies together, Fabric shortcut verified afterward with the junk_rows query already documented in the consumer contract.

  4. Stand up the learner as propose-only

    Let it run alongside a few evaluation cycles and produce proposals Carl declines, until its backtest numbers earn confidence.

  5. Split the instruction file done

    Seven parts under instructions/, indexed from the root file, loaded per run type.

  6. Move the orchestrator to Haiku

    The remaining step, and now unblocked: every mechanical operation is a script, and the instruction load is scoped per run. This is the change most likely to need iteration, which is why it goes last.
